Lately, I’ve found myself thinking less about AI and more about people.

That probably sounds strange given how much of the business conversation today revolves around technology. Everywhere I look, there seems to be another prediction about what AI will replace, which industries will be disrupted and how dramatically business will change over the next decade. I understand the fascination. The pace of change is extraordinary, and there is no doubt that AI will reshape the way businesses operate. Yet the more I listen to those conversations, the more my attention drifts elsewhere. I keep finding myself thinking about the businesses I’ve admired most throughout my career and wondering what it was that made them successful in the first place.

What’s interesting is that very few of those businesses became successful because they had access to something nobody else could access. Most weren’t operating with unique technology. They weren’t protected by some secret advantage that competitors couldn’t replicate. In many cases, they were doing things that looked relatively simple from the outside. They served customers well. They built trust. They delivered consistently. They created strong teams. They developed reputations that took years to earn and minutes to verify. When I think about the businesses that have stood the test of time, those are the qualities that come to mind first, not the tools they happened to be using at the time.

Perhaps that’s why I’ve become increasingly convinced that the businesses most likely to thrive in an AI world won’t necessarily be the ones talking about AI the most. They’ll be the ones that understand how to combine technology with something deeper. Technology can increase speed. It can improve efficiency. It can remove repetitive work. What it doesn’t do particularly well is create trust, exercise judgement or build meaningful relationships. Those things still belong to people. In fact, I sometimes wonder whether those qualities become even more valuable as technology becomes more powerful.

I’ve noticed this in my own behaviour as a customer. There are countless situations where I could choose a cheaper option, a faster option or a more convenient option. Yet I often find myself returning to businesses and people I already trust. Sometimes I’ve worked with them for years. Sometimes I’ve known them for decades. The decision isn’t always logical when viewed through a purely commercial lens. It’s based on confidence. I know how they operate. I know what standards they hold themselves to. I know they’ll tell me the truth even when it’s uncomfortable. That trust has value, and I don’t think technology changes that.

In many ways, I think we’re moving towards a future where human judgement becomes more important rather than less. As information becomes increasingly available and content becomes easier to create, the real challenge shifts from finding answers to knowing which answers matter. We already live in a world where there is no shortage of information. What people often struggle with is interpretation. They want context. They want perspective. They want someone who understands the nuances of a situation and can help them make sense of it. That’s a very different skill from simply providing information.

The businesses that stand out to me today are often the ones that understand this distinction. They aren’t trying to compete solely on efficiency because efficiency eventually becomes available to everyone. They aren’t trying to compete solely on information because information becomes increasingly accessible. Instead, they’re building businesses around trust, expertise, relationships and reputation. They’re creating experiences that feel human at a time when so much of the world feels automated.

I also think there is something else happening beneath the surface that doesn’t get discussed enough. As technology makes it easier for businesses to produce more content, more communication and more output, authenticity becomes easier to recognise. People can usually tell when something feels genuine and when it doesn’t. They can sense when a business truly understands its customers and when it’s simply following a trend. The tools may become more sophisticated, but human intuition hasn’t disappeared. If anything, I think people are becoming more sensitive to what feels real because they are surrounded by so much that doesn’t.

Over the years, I’ve spent a lot of time thinking about competitive advantage. Early in my career, I assumed it was largely about capabilities. If you had better systems, better processes or better resources, you would win. While those things certainly matter, I’ve gradually come to believe that many of the strongest competitive advantages are much harder to measure. Culture is difficult to copy. Trust is difficult to copy. Long-term relationships are difficult to copy. A strong reputation is difficult to copy. Those things take years to build and they don’t suddenly become less valuable because new technology arrives.

When I picture the businesses that will thrive over the next decade, I don’t imagine organisations that rely entirely on people or entirely on technology. I imagine businesses that understand how to use both. They’ll embrace AI because ignoring it would make little sense. They’ll automate what should be automated. They’ll improve efficiency where efficiency can be improved. They’ll move faster than businesses that resist change. At the same time, they’ll remain deeply human in the areas that matter most. They’ll invest in relationships. They’ll develop strong cultures. They’ll earn trust. They’ll understand their customers at a level that goes beyond data.

The more I reflect on it, the more I think the future belongs to businesses that understand this balance. Not businesses that see technology as a replacement for human value, but businesses that use technology to amplify it. Because when everyone eventually has access to similar tools, similar capabilities and similar efficiencies, the differentiators begin to shift elsewhere. They shift towards judgement, character, relationships, trust and reputation. Those qualities have always mattered in business, and I suspect they always will.

For all the discussion about what AI will change, I find myself equally interested in what it won’t change. People will still want to work with people they trust. Customers will still remember how a business made them feel. Teams will still follow leaders they respect. Reputations will still be built one interaction at a time. Technology may alter the way we work, but I don’t believe it changes the fundamental reasons people choose one business over another. If anything, it simply shines a brighter light on them.

When people talk about success, the conversation usually revolves around visible things. Revenue, business growth, promotions, investments, properties, recognition and achievements tend to dominate the discussion. Those are the things people can see from the outside, so naturally they become the things we talk about.

What interests me more these days are the quieter changes that happen underneath the surface. The things that don’t show up on a résumé, a LinkedIn profile or a balance sheet. The things you rarely notice while they’re happening but become impossible to ignore when you look back over a decade or two.

One of the biggest surprises for me has been how much success changes you as a person, often in ways that have nothing to do with money or career progression. When I was younger, I assumed success would mostly change my circumstances. I thought it would create more opportunities, more freedom and perhaps a greater sense of security. What I didn’t appreciate was how much it would change the way I viewed the world. The older I’ve become, the more I’ve realised that growth isn’t just about building businesses. It’s also about becoming a different person from the one who started building them.

I think that’s why certain relationships naturally evolve over time. This is probably one of the least discussed parts of success because it can feel uncomfortable to talk about. There is often an assumption that if relationships change, somebody must be at fault. In my experience, that’s rarely the case. More often, people simply grow in different directions. The things that matter to you at twenty-five may not be the things that matter to you at forty. The conversations that energised you years ago may no longer hold your attention in the same way. The priorities that shape your life inevitably influence the people you spend your time with.

When I was younger, I had a much broader definition of friendship. As the years have passed, that definition has become much narrower but also much deeper. Today, I have a very small circle of friends. It’s not something that happened intentionally. It simply happened as life became fuller. Between family, businesses, travel and everything else that comes with this stage of life, time has become one of the most valuable things I have. I’ve become far more selective about who I spend it with. Not because I think I’m better than anyone else, but because I’ve realised how much our environment influences the way we think.

These days, I find myself drawn towards people who challenge me in positive ways. People who make me think differently. People who inspire me to grow. People who have experiences, perspectives or knowledge that expand my own understanding of the world. Some of those people are founders. Some are investors. Some are parents. Some have built extraordinary lives in completely different fields. What they have in common is that I almost always leave those conversations feeling like I’ve learned something.

Looking back, I think success has made me more curious than ambitious. That’s probably not something I would have expected to say twenty years ago. When I was younger, achievement felt incredibly important. I was focused on building, progressing and creating opportunities. Those things still matter to me, but curiosity has become equally valuable. I’m fascinated by how people think, how they solve problems, how they navigate challenges and how they build meaningful lives. Some of the most enjoyable conversations I have today have nothing to do with business at all. They’re simply opportunities to see the world through somebody else’s lens.

Success can create a strange sense of perspective. Problems that once felt enormous no longer seem quite so significant. You live through enough challenges to realise that most situations are temporary. You experience enough uncertainty to understand that certainty is largely an illusion. You make enough mistakes to recognise that perfection is neither achievable nor particularly useful. In many ways, experience softens the edges of ambition. Not because you care less, but because you begin to understand what truly deserves your energy.

Perhaps that’s why I think the most meaningful parts of success are often invisible. They don’t generate headlines. They don’t attract attention. They don’t make impressive stories at networking events. They show up in smaller ways. They show up in the confidence that comes from experience. They show up in the quality of your relationships. They show up in the conversations you choose to have, the people you choose to spend time with and the values you become less willing to compromise as you get older.

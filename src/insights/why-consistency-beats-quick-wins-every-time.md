I was looking through old photos recently, trying to find a picture of my son from years ago.

Like most people, my camera roll is a complete mess. Thousands of photos. Screenshots I’ll never use. Videos I forgot I took. Random moments that seemed important at the time.

Somewhere between all of that, I found photos from different periods of my life that I hadn’t looked at in years. There were photos from when the kids were little. Photos from our first office. Photos from apartments I’ve lived in, businesses I’ve started, trips I’ve taken, projects I’ve worked on and completely forgotten about.

What struck me wasn’t how different everything looked. It was how gradual all the change had been.

Looking back now, the distance between those photos feels enormous. Entire chapters of life sit between them. Businesses were built. Relationships changed. Children grew up. New opportunities appeared. Other opportunities disappeared.

Yet I can remember living through many of those periods and feeling like nothing much was happening.

That’s the strange thing about progress.

When we’re inside it, it often feels invisible.

I think that’s one of the reasons so many people become obsessed with quick wins. They’re easier to recognise. They give us a story to tell ourselves. A sudden spike in revenue, a viral post, a promotion, a new client or a lucky break. Those moments feel satisfying because they create the illusion that progress happens in big, obvious leaps.

But when I look back over my own life, very little of what matters arrived that way.

My career wasn’t built in a year. My businesses weren’t built in a year. Even my confidence wasn’t built in a year. The things that have had the biggest impact on my life developed so gradually that I barely noticed them happening at the time. Looking back now, I can see how much changed, but while I was living through it, most of those periods felt remarkably ordinary.

A lot of progress hides itself inside repetition. The same commute. The same conversations. The same routines. The same decisions made day after day. We tend to think transformation should feel dramatic while it’s happening, but in my experience it rarely does. More often, it feels like showing up on days when you don’t particularly feel like it, continuing when the results aren’t obvious, and trusting that the effort will eventually compound into something meaningful.

I see the same pattern when I speak with founders. Many are far further ahead than they realise. The challenge is that they’re standing too close to their own progress to see it. Growth rarely feels like growth while it’s happening. It feels like uncertainty. It feels like repetition. It feels like doing the work without immediate evidence that it’s making a difference.

The irony is that those are often the moments that matter most.

When I look back through those old photos now, that’s probably what stands out to me. Life changed. Business changed. The children grew up. I changed. Not because of one defining moment, but because of thousands of ordinary days that didn’t seem particularly important at the time.

Maybe that’s what consistency really is.

A collection of ordinary days that only becomes extraordinary when viewed in hindsight.

The older I get, the more I realise how much of life comes down to trust.

Not just in business, but everywhere.

Who we hire. Who we partner with. Who we invest with. Who we take advice from. Who we call when something important goes wrong.

I’ve been thinking about this a lot recently because we’re living in a world where information has never been easier to access, yet certainty often feels harder to find.

Every day we’re exposed to endless content, opinions, reviews, podcasts, articles, videos and now AI-generated answers. Within seconds, we can find information on almost anything. In theory, that should make decision-making easier.

I’m not convinced it does.

If anything, I think many people feel more overwhelmed than ever. There are so many opinions that it becomes difficult to know whose opinion matters. So many experts that it’s hard to know who has genuine expertise. So much information that people end up searching for something entirely different.

Confidence.

Not confidence in the information itself, but confidence in the person helping them interpret it.

I notice this in my own life. There are plenty of decisions where I could spend hours researching every option and analysing every detail. Sometimes I do. More often, I find myself turning to someone whose judgement I trust. Not because they’re always right, but because I know how they think. I know their intentions. I know they’ll tell me the truth, even when it’s not what I want to hear.

That distinction feels increasingly valuable.

I’ve seen the same thing happen in business. Some of the strongest client relationships we’ve built weren’t created because we had information nobody else had. They were built because clients trusted us to be honest. Sometimes that meant recommending a smaller solution. Sometimes it meant admitting we weren’t the right fit. Those conversations don’t always create immediate wins, but they often create long-term relationships.

That’s the interesting thing about trust. It compounds.

One honest conversation becomes another. One positive experience becomes another. Over time, people stop evaluating every interaction because they already know what to expect from you.

You can buy attention. You can buy visibility. You can buy traffic.

Trust doesn’t work that way.

It takes years to build and only moments to lose.

The more technology advances, the more convinced I become that trust will be one of the most valuable assets a person or business can possess. AI will make information more abundant. Content will become easier to create. Expertise will become harder to distinguish from noise.

When that happens, people won’t just be looking for answers.

They’ll be looking for people they believe.

When I think about the businesses that will thrive over the next decade, I don’t necessarily think about those with the biggest audiences or the most sophisticated technology. I think about the businesses that consistently do what they say they’re going to do. The leaders whose names carry credibility. The people who earn trust repeatedly over long periods of time.

Technology will continue changing.

Human behaviour will continue evolving.

But trust feels different.

For all the things that are becoming easier to replicate, trust remains one of the few things that still has to be earned.
